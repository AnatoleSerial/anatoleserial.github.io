## What is a "Lancer Garage"?

Traditionally, a "Garage" is either a place where vehicles are sheltered, or a commercial business in which vehicles are repaired. The word seems thematic to Lancer itself, having mechs and all, but in truth, for this instance, it comes from another source. In **Vampire The Requiem**'s "Requiem Chronicler's Guide", many writers, some who were or eventually become well-known TTRPG authors and developers, explored how to add or remove elements from a Vampire game to create more unique and interesting games. It is a beautiful book, if anything because it shows a blueprint on how much you can break a game for the sake of fun. The section that broke the game the most was called *Monster Garage*, written by Jared Sorensen, and it's fantastic. It dystills and subverts a lot of what you would expect from a Vampire: The Requiem game, turning it less from a game of monsters clinging to their humanity and more into a lean, mean game of monsters reveling in their inhumanity. Kinda like "The Lost Boys", but more *gothic*. It's stuck with me all these years.

So *my* Lancer Garage is not like that "Monster Garage", but more like the book in which it appeared. Still, the sentiment is there: in this place I plan to break down, put together, and fix all sorts of things surrounding the role of the GM in Lancer. Because, well, from a GM's perspective, Lancer is the kind of game that *really* seems to ask *a lot* out of the GM. But *does it*?

## Why are we here?

Running **Lancer** is challenging. Over at Pilot NET, almost every day there is at least one message, from a prospective Game Master, who is asking other GMs for help on how to run the game. They always get a lot of assistance, because in general GMs love to help. This is a good thing. 

On the same channel, GMs of varying experience show their work, trade anecdotes and tips on how to run certain types of encounters, and so on. This is a good thing.

I have read a lot of what Lancer GMs have to say. A lot of their prep methods, the things they are anxious about, the things they struggle with. It is a lot. But, I have also noticed some common *refrains*, things that many GMs say and/or do, that make me think, *doesn't this kinda SUCK in some way*?

Many GMs spend a lot of time doing *unnecessary* things. This is true for TTRPGs in general, and even more so when the GMs lack experience. Once you start getting some experience, your first instinct is to improve, and that means changing things.

**Don't**. Your first instinct should be, instead of looking *just* at what has gone wrong and can be improved, into what has *worked* and keep it, and what *hasn't* worked and just blast it away.

Sidenote: I play D&D with a group of GMs. And by that I mean, most of us are GMs and we run games for each other. One of them is the most inexperienced of us: his first D&D edition was 5E! He's the one who introduced many of us to Foundry, and at the beginning of his campaign spent an *inordinate* amount of time beautifying his game. For the few sessions that his setup worked, it worked *wonderfully*. Then Foundry updated and everything broke, so then he fixed enough for us to play, and play we did. We had just the same amount of fun, regardless of how automated and beautiful the VTT was. Over time he's found ways to run games more flexibly. He'll get better. We GMs tend to get excited and do a lot of things that we think are cool but instead make us spend too much time on things that will not be enjoyed by our players. I truly believe we all can get better at this. Back to Lancer, though.

A lot of Lancer GMs over at Pilot NET show their work. You can tell they are putting a lot of effort into maps every session, trying to design intricate combats that may or may not happen. Or planning a wonderful narrative session with the hope, the expectation, the desire of, if all goes well, not having a contact until the *next* session.

Doesn't all of this sound... A little *dreadful*?

### The Lancer Corebook preaches you one thing but asks of you another.

After you've been GMing for a long time, you realize this is *foolish*. Even the Lancer Corebook says it: *relinquish control* of the narrative, GMs! Let the players try and break things. Make their choices *matter*. The more *specific* things you prepare, the more you are *not* letting the narrative flow where it must.

Surely you can see the problems that arise here, yes? The book tells you to play fast and loose, and when combat comes, to whip out a map, some NPCs, and a sitrep to keep things spicy. Except:

- *Maps require time to prepare*. Throwing together a quick map is feasible for smaller skirmishes, but the *expected* average Lancer map is a tad too large for that. Which means that it might be better for the GM to prepare in advance... Except, well, if we're "rolling with the punches", *how much should we prepare in advance?*
- *NPCs are not always easy & fast to make*. This is a side-effect of Lancer having a "bestiary" in which all enemies are made of legos: you can add and remove things from them. A *generic encounter* is easy to make, but if you're designing a set of enemies that fit the narrative, *you have to plan ahead*.
- *Sitreps will not necessarily fit the narrative*, which is a big thing. If the combat is just a bunch of no-names pew-pew-ing against the PCs, that's not fun. Similarly, if the sitrep doesn't quite match the narrative, it will be jarring to run it. The current state of Sitreps as they are on the corebook is simply *insufficient* -- it's a good start though.

Those are *some* of the most salient points that grind down Lancer prep. They're not all, and they're mostly from the perspective of one GM (me) who has both struggled and observed the ways in which other GMs have struggled. We can do better.

And, to note, this ain't the book's fault. It's a product of its own inheritance from the games that influence it. But just as Lancer inherits the language of TTRPGs and Wargaming, it also inherits its *ethos*: **Hack the Game. Make it Yours.**

### Breaking Things and Fixing Things

If we want to run Lancer with a leaner (even [lazy](https://shop.slyflourish.com/products/return-of-the-lazy-dungeon-master)) approach, we need to be willing to take a look at things as they are in the corebook, *break them apart* and then *put things back together*.

Here is my list of things I am cooking. You can think of it as a *roadmap* of things I want to develop, test, then write about. 

- **F\*ck The Mission Structure**: We don't need this as a *codified game framework*. I'll tell you why, in time.
- **You're Doing Reserves Wrong**: We all are. Because the book tells us so. This ties to the Mission Structure, so it makes sense we're also kicking some dust in this area.
- **Mech Combat as Conflict Resolution**: Or, how to run *less* combats. Or *more* combats but *less* tactical combats. You don't need to whip out the map on every encounter in a mech.
- **Factions: Or, Fiction-first NPCs**: Two parts to this. The first is how to define a faction within your setting, and the second is how to give it *extra* personality. Will this require new systems? *Maaaaaaybe?*
- **You only need One Map**: Or, "how to spend a lot of time preparing 'maps' for multiple sessions without worrying about the narrative while providing variety".
- **Sitrep Chopshop**: Once you've learn to run with Sitreps, you must learn how to run without Sitreps. By taking them apart. Use their parts instead, and connect them to the narrative.
- **Spicing up Combats with Narrative Effects**: Temp title. A framework seeking to enable players to do More Things in combat other than shooting and hacking each other.
- **NPC Buddies**: Examining NPCs-As-Companions. That's all I have. For now.
- **Running for Smaller Groups**: Lancer combat was not tuned for groups of less than 3 players.
