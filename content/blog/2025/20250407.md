## Introduction

### What is LANCER?

**Lancer** is a Tabletop Roleplaying game in which the player's character are exceptional Mech pilots, riding customized machines into battle in a far distant future as humanity expands into the stars, with all the opportunities and issues this entails.

Lancer's setting *rocks*. Most of humanity lives under the banner of Union, a pan-galactic governments with an "utopia-in-progress" set of flexible ideals. While Union provides stability, not everyone wants to abide by their idealism, which brings conflict. Thus, Lancer's *fiction* provides ample opportunities to tell stories with social, political, and emotional depth. It fulfills the trope of The Mech as a Symbol, a Metaphor.

Lancer's system is... *complicated*. On one hand, it has a *Narrative* system for scenes in which the player characters interact with the world outside of their machines. It is lightweight and relatively easy to grasp.

 On the other hand, it has a *Mech Combat* system. It's *crunchy*: mechs come equipped with weapons and systems that give them plenty of options during combat. It can be overwhelming, but once you find your stride the turns *flow*. It becomes *quite elegant*.

### Why am I writing this?

Unlike the vast majority of TTRPG game masters out there, I did not roll a D20 until I had already GM'd around a dozen stories. My experience as a GM involved chunking a bunch of ten-sided dice and counting the ones that turned up higher than a given number. Not a good system if you want *crunchy* combat, but a good system if you want rules that help adjudicate character aptitude, or systems that aid in *rulings*.

Eventually I played other games, including at least 3 editions of Dungeons and Dragons. Eventually fell in love with Lancer's settings and tried, for *years*, to understand its mechanics, to no avail. Then I tried harder, got a group of people willing to let *me* try harder, and thus I became a Lancer GM.

Running Lancer is *hard* when you come from a background that eschews deep strategy in favor of dramatic narratives. Even if Lancer has a "Narrative" system, one of its biggest issues is that while its Narrative and Mech Combat have *some* common elements, they are otherwise *very disconnected*. 

 This disconnect is not necessarily a bad thing: the grittiness of the combat is exciting and can lead to some tense moments. The problem is that moving to and from combat feels unnatural, like entering and exiting combat in a Computer RPG. The disconnect hinders *narrative flow*.
 
 And to further complicate matters, if a new GM wants to learn how to run Lancer and is worried that Narrative scenes might be too short, or combat might be too long, or if the players decide to enter a combat that was not well-planned in advance, a common piece of wisdom in the Lancer GM community is that in case of an unexpected combat, it is better to *end the session right there and then*, then spend *the entire next session* running said combat after the GM has had the chance to make all the prep for it. This **kills flow**. It's boring. It's *disappointing*.

I am writing this as a way to spitball/brainstorm ideas to run a smoother Lancer. To be able to run a session with as little prep as possible, with as much improvisation as possible. To enable Player-driven fiction, while maintaining the exciting elements of Mech Combat and tying it more closely tied to Narrative mechanics.

## Rethinking Lancer's Narrative Framing

Lancer is a game of Mech pilots. These pilots can have a wide variety of backgrounds, skills and talents. This encourages making diverse pilots from all walks of life. Your Lancer game is their story.

Lancer is a game of Mech combat. Mech combat is rigidly defined, with defined rules and expectations. It is "crunchy" and tactical. It is also quite fun.

And *extremely limiting*. This section is about how the Lancer corebook frames its narrative, and the issues/obstacles this framing introduces.

### Lancer's Standard Operating Procedure

Lancer's narrative is built around **Mech Pilots** undertaking **Missions**. By the book, each Mission is composed of phases:
1. Briefing
2. Preparation
3. Reserves
4. Boots on the Ground
5. Ending/Debriefing

Once a Mission's over, the players can Level Up. Then the process repeats itself.

During a mission, characters are either in Narrative Play or Mech Combat. These two have vastly different rules. Combat during Narrative Play is resolved through *Skill Checks*, while in Mech Combat everything is resolved through *Attack Rolls*.

In between Missions, there's **Downtime** in the form of **Structured** and **Freeform** play. The book provides examples of *Downtime Actions* that Pilots can do to get *Reserves* they can bring into the mission. Those actions are Skill Checks.

Mech Combat initiates the moment anyone carries out hostile actions. 

#### What this means

From a Pilot's perspective, everything that matters in Lancer is Missions. Those are the only things that lead to character progression. Everything else is designed to feed into Missions. The GM's job, outside of Missions, is to give the Pilots *Downtime* Structured  activities that give them Reserves, and maybe some Freeform stuff thrown in there, as a treat. This kind of structure encourages the GM to *offer/initiate* these, so the Pilots respond by choosing the available options.

If you've been paying attention, you will notice that a lot of these elements, while useful, really constrain what you can do with the system. We are not getting rid of them, *per se*, but rather we are removing them from the core assumptions, and replacing them with a framing that, while generic, can be used to better design a Lancer story.

### What if...?

#### What if No Missions?

Let us start by erasing the word **Mission** from our set of game assumptions, and replace it with a more useful concept: a *Story*. A good ol' narrative with a beginning, a middle, and an end. As a GM, you begin a story by giving the players a situation that is relevant to their Pilots. This, of course, can be a (lowercase-m) mission handed out by someone else, but it could also be a more personal story. We are just getting rid of the **Mission** framework entirely.

This means that, after an initial presentation of the situation, the GM's job is to *stop initiating*. The players must *act* according to that initial situation. The overall narrative emerges from the Pilots' actions; even if the GM has ideas of where they want the story to go, the ones who engage with and change the world *are* the players, through their pilots.

#### What if No Downtime?

Let's also get rid of **Downtime** as a game assumption. It is tied to (uppercase-M) Missions, so it naturally needs to go: codifying it as a "game phase" is pointless if we want the narrative to *flow*. In the corebook, everything  detailed as being part of "Downtime" becomes part of narrative play in general, without distinction. 

This means that the GM has to start a Story in a way that gives players enough freedom to persue their Pilots' own goals and interest, both as individuals and as a group. This pursuit can (an likely should!) tie into whatever plot the GM is planning to throw the pilots into, as well as to provide them with suitable rewards (permanent equipment, Reserves). The most important part here is player freedom: you can encourage the players to seek things like Reserves, but not as their own, codified "Downtime Action". 

#### What if we need Skills during Mech Combat?

Here is the thing: the game divides Narrative Play and Mech Combat as *wholly separate things*. But *are they, really*? Pilots, with their *Skills* and *Backgrounds*, are inside the Mechs. That means that, Rules As Written, any action that is *not* outlined in the Mech Combat section, cannot be resolved with Pilot Skills.

**That's *bollocks***.

During Mech Combat, the players can try any action. Literally any action. *They should not try to limit themselves to what is outlined in the book*. Anything that is not outlined in the Mech Combat rules, should be doable with either a *Mech Skill Check* if the action is done through a Mech, or a *Pilot Skill Check* if it is the Pilot doing the thing. This means that something such as "I try to flirt with the enemy commander, convince them to stand down" is a valid (if *risky*) roll.

Skill Checks from Rulings will usually be Full Actions unless they are real quick, and as usual you have to accurately express the consequences of failure after the player states their intent but before they roll. In Combat, the consequences (On success or failure) of non-combat actions must always be something that changes the state of the combat: either Statuses or Conditions, depending on the results.

And hey, GMs, here's a tip: you can make up new Statuses and Conditions. Always make sure you specify the conditions in which they are imposed and lost, too! For future consistency.

### Summary

In short, so far we have done nothing particularly useful. We have just reframed Lancer's core assumptions as more general *narrative* assumptions. This gives us freedom to explore how the game's Narrative and Mech Combat systems tick, and how they interact with each other.
