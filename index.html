Latest post: Encounters in LANCER

What is LANCER?

Lancer is a Tabletop Roleplaying game in which the player's character are exceptional Mech pilots, riding customized machines into battle in a far distant future as humanity expands into the stars, with all the opportunities and issues this entails.

Lancer's setting rocks. Most of humanity lives under the banner of Union, a pan-galactic governments with an "utopia-in-progress" set of flexible ideals. While Union provides stability, not everyone wants to abide by their idealism, which brings conflict. Thus, Lancer's fiction provides ample opportunities to tell stories with social, political, and emotional depth. It fulfills the trope of The Mech as a Symbol, a Metaphor.

Lancer's system is... complicated. On one hand, it has a Narrative system for scenes in which the player characters interact with the world outside of their machines. It is lightweight and relatively easy to grasp.

On the other hand, it has a Mech Combat system. It's crunchy: mechs come equipped with weapons and systems that give them plenty of options during combat. It can be overwhelming, but once you find your stride the turns flow. It becomes elegant.

Why am I writing this?

One of its biggest issues is that, well, the Narrative and Mech Combat have some common elements, but are otherwise disconnected. Narratively you need to roll 10 or above to succeed, with certain roles having degrees of success (or, Full Success vs Success With Consequences), yet a combat's roll succeess depends on Evasion/E-Defense and it is entirely pass or fail.

This disconnect is not necessarily a bad thing. The problem is that it has also created a culture in which these two are so disconnected, a common piece of wisdom in the Lancer GM community is that if the players enter a fight you don't have planned, it is better to end the session right there and then, then spend the entire next session running said combat. Which is, frankly, ridiculous.

I am writing this as a way to spitball/brainstorm ideas to run a smoother Lancer. The objective is to present options that enable Player-driven fiction, with Mech Combat more closely tied to Narrative mechanics.

Rethinking Lancer's Narrative Framing

Lancer is a game of Mech pilots. These pilots can have a wide variety of backgrounds, skills and talents. This encourages making diverse pilots from all walks of life. Your Lancer game is their story.

Lancer is a game of Mech combat. Mech combat is rigidly defined, with defined rules and expectations. It is "crunchy" and tactical. It is also quite fun.

And extremely limiting. This section is about how the Lancer corebook frames its narrative, and the issues/obstacles this framing introduces.

Lancer's Standard Operating Procedure

Lancer's narrative is built around Mech Pilots undertaking Missions. By the book, each Mission is composed of phases:

  1. Briefing
  2. Preparation
  3. Reserves
  4. Boots on the Ground
  5. Ending/Debriefing

Once a Mission's over, the players can Level Up. Then the process repeats itself.

During a mission, characters are either in Narrative Play or Mech Combat. These two have vastly different rules. Combat during Narrative Play is resolved through Skill Checks, while in Mech Combat everything is resolved through Attack Rolls.

In between Missions, there's Downtime in the form of Structured and Freeform play. The book provides examples of Downtime Actions that Pilots can do to get Reserves they can bring into the mission. Those actions are Skill Checks.

Mech Combat initiates the moment anyone carries out hostile actions.

What this means

From a Pilot's perspective, everything that matters in Lancer is Missions. Those are the only things that lead to character progression. Everything else is designed to feed into Missions. The GM's job, outside of Missions, is to give the Pilots Downtime Structured activities that give them Reserves, and maybe some Freeform stuff thrown in there, as a treat. This kind of structure encourages the GM to offer/initiate these, so the Pilots respond by choosing the available options.

What if we just don't...?

What if No Missions?

Let us start by erasing the word Mission from our set of game assumptions, and replace it with a more useful concept: a Story. A good ol' narrative with a beginning, a middle, and an end. As a GM, you begin a story by giving the players a situation that is relevant to their Pilots. This, of course, can be a (lowercase-m) mission handed out by someone else, but it could also be a more personal story. We are just getting rid of the Mission framework entirely.

This means that, after an initial presentation of the situation, the GM's job is to stop initiating. The players must act according to that initial situation. The overall narrative emerges from the Pilots' actions.

What if No Downtime?

Let's also get rid of Downtime as a game assumption. It is tied to (uppercase-M) Missions, so it needs to go. "Downtime" is part of the narrative, so codifying it as a "phase" is pointless if we want the narrative to flow. Everything detailed as being part of "Downtime" just becomes part of the narrative, period.

This means that the GM has to start a Story in a way that gives players enough freedom to persue their Pilots' own goals, both as individuals and as a group. This pursuit can (an likely should!) tie into whatever plot the GM is planning to throw the pilots into, as well as to provide them with Reserves. If players are looking for specific Reserves, and there are no obstacles for their acquisition, then just hand it over; otherwise, narrate and roll as usual. Do note that not all players will want to look for particular reserves: as a GM, you should still encourage Reserves, but not push them as "Downtime actions".

What if we need Skills during Mech Combat?

Here is the thing: the game divides Narrative Play and Mech Combat as wholly separate things. But are they, really? Pilots, with their Skills and Backgrounds, are inside the Mechs. That means that, Rules As Written, any action that is not outlined in the Mech Combat section, cannot be resolved with Pilot Skills.

That's bollocks.

During Mech Combat, the players can try any action. Literally any action. They should not try to limit themselves to what is outlined in the book. Anything that is not outlined in the Mech Combat rules, should be doable with either a Mech Skill Check if the action is done through a Mech, or a Pilot Skill Check if it is the Pilot doing the thing. This means that something such as "I try to flirt with the enemy commander, convince them to stand down" is a valid (if risky) roll. In TTRPG parlance, these kinds of out-of-the-rules decisions are called Rulings.

Skill Checks from Rulings will usually be Full Actions unless they are real quick, and as usual you have to accurately express the consequences of failure after the player states their intent but before they roll. In Combat, the consequences must always be something that changes the state of the combat: either Statuses or Conditions, depending on the results.

And hey, GMs, here's a tip: you can make up new Statuses and Conditions. Always make sure you specify the conditions in which they are imposed and lost, too!
